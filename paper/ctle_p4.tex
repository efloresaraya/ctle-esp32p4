\begin{abstract}
Deploying Tiny Language Models (TinyLMs) on low-power IoT microcontrollers is
challenging due to tight memory budgets and the runtime overhead of weight
dequantization during inference.
This paper presents the Compressed Tensor--LUT Engine (CTLE), a 4-bit
weight-sharing representation and streaming runtime that executes
transformer-based models directly from a tensor-LUT format, without
reconstructing full-precision weight tensors in RAM.
Each weight matrix is decomposed into a small learned codebook (LUT) of $K=16$
centroids and a matrix of packed 4-bit indices; CTLE performs
matrix--vector products by streaming indices and applying on-the-fly LUT
lookups.
To maximize quantization quality, we propose and compare three codebook
learning strategies: K-means clustering, a Genetic Algorithm (GA) with
BLX-$\alpha$ crossover and warm start, and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
with inertia-weight decay---both metaheuristic methods accelerated with
Numba JIT compilation.

We target a 15M-parameter TinyStories transformer and evaluate all methods on
a 10-sentence corpus.
PSO achieves perplexity (PPL) of 15.93 vs.\ 19.23 for K-means---a
\textbf{17.2\,\%} reduction---at identical 7.61\,MB footprint (7.98$\times$
compression vs.\ FP32), requiring only 64\,B of LUT storage per matrix during
inference.
As INT4 baselines: uniform INT4 collapses (PPL\,=\,11070) due to its single
global scale, while block-wise INT4 ($G{=}32$) achieves PPL\,=\,10.71 at the
cost of 25\,\% higher footprint (9.51\,MB) and per-group scale overhead.
We further implement a streaming C inference engine for the ESP32-P4 Nano
(RISC-V HP\,@360\,MHz, 32\,MB PSRAM).
Hardware benchmarks across all formats reveal that INT4BW is
\textbf{31\,\%} slower than INT4U and CTLE (1{,}356 vs.\
1{,}038\,ms/tok) due to per-group scale reads from PSRAM, while
CTLE achieves \textbf{0.965\,tok/s} with the LUT resident in registers,
21.5\,MB PSRAM free, and prefill throughput coherent with generation speed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) has substantially improved
natural language generation and understanding, but at the cost of rapid growth
in parameter count and compute---conflicting with the memory, bandwidth, and
energy budgets of low-power Internet-of-Things (IoT) nodes.
Typical microcontrollers used in IoT endpoints, such as the ESP32-P4, provide
limited on-chip SRAM, external QSPI Flash, and external PSRAM, but offer
neither high-throughput floating-point compute nor the memory bandwidth needed
to repeatedly materialize large weight tensors.
Deploying non-trivial language models on such devices requires aggressive
compression, or offloading inference to the cloud---which reduces autonomy and
raises privacy and availability concerns.

TinyML research addresses microcontroller constraints through model compression,
post-training quantization, efficient kernels, and MCU-oriented inference
frameworks~\cite{Saha2022,Novac2021,Elhanashi2024}.
Compact recurrent and sequence models demonstrate that small networks can be
tailored to strict resource envelopes~\cite{Kusupati2018,Kannan2021,Cheng2024}.
In parallel, GPU-targeted 4-bit quantization methods such as
GPTQ~\cite{frantar2023gptq} and SqueezeLLM~\cite{kim2024squeezellm} achieve
near-lossless compression on large LLMs, but require GPU VRAM for inference
and do not target streaming execution on Flash-only microcontrollers.

This work introduces the Compressed Tensor--LUT Engine (CTLE), an inference
engine that executes transformer layers directly from a 4-bit weight-sharing
representation on ESP32-P4-class microcontrollers.
Each weight matrix is represented by a tensor-wise codebook of $K=16$
real-valued centroids and a matrix of 4-bit indices, packed as two indices per
byte and stored in QSPI Flash.
Rather than dequantizing weights into SRAM, CTLE performs a fused
Tensor--LUT computation in which indices are decoded on-the-fly inside a
streaming matrix--vector kernel.

A key contribution of this paper is the application of \emph{metaheuristic
codebook optimization}. Standard K-means converges to local minima that
may be suboptimal for large, non-Gaussian weight distributions.
We propose GA and PSO variants with warm start from K-means, Numba JIT
acceleration, and stochastic fitness subsampling, enabling high-quality
codebook search within minutes on a laptop CPU.
PSO, in particular, improves over K-means by $-$14.9\,\% relative MSE on
the critical embedding tensor (9.2\,M weights), translating to
$-$17.2\,\% PPL reduction at zero additional memory cost.

We instantiate CTLE on a 15M-parameter TinyStories language model and
demonstrate end-to-end on-device inference on an ESP32-P4 Nano, achieving
0.965\,tok/s. We compare against INT4 uniform and INT4 block-wise baselines
(our own implementations) and GPU-targeting methods from the literature.

\paragraph{Main contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item CTLE, a firmware-level inference engine for ESP32-P4-class
    microcontrollers that streams packed 4-bit weight-sharing tensors from
    external QSPI Flash and computes transformer matvecs without materializing
    dense dequantized weight buffers in SRAM/PSRAM.

    \item A fused Tensor--LUT matrix--vector kernel that decodes packed 4-bit
    indices on-the-fly and performs lookup-and-accumulate, reducing transient
    weight-expansion memory from $\mathcal{O}(MN)$ floating-point storage to
    $\mathcal{O}(1)$ additional working state, while retaining a 16-entry LUT
    per tensor.

    \item \textbf{Metaheuristic codebook optimization}: GA (BLX-$\alpha$
    crossover, tournament selection, elitism) and PSO (inertia-weight decay
    $\omega{:}\ 0.9\!\to\!0.4$, warm start from K-means) with Numba JIT
    acceleration---both achieving compression in under 4 minutes on a laptop.

    \item A full comparative evaluation including INT4 Uniform, INT4 Block-wise
    ($G{=}32$), CTLE+K-means, CTLE+GA, and CTLE+PSO, with hardware
    benchmark on ESP32-P4 Nano at 0.965\,tok/s.

    \item A multi-format C firmware engine supporting TAG\_F32,
    TAG\_CTLE, TAG\_INT4U, and TAG\_INT4BW, enabling fair on-device
    comparison across all schemes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{TinyML and MCU deployment}
Saha et al.\ review ML on microcontroller hardware, emphasizing that SRAM
capacity, energy, and bandwidth are primary bottlenecks~\cite{Saha2022}.
Novac et al.\ present MicroAI for training, quantizing, and deploying networks
on low-power MCUs~\cite{Novac2021}.
Elhanashi et al.\ survey TinyML advances for IoT devices, focusing on
compression methods and accelerators~\cite{Elhanashi2024}.
Sakr proposes LUT-based binarized networks for tiny devices~\cite{Sakr2023}.
Cerioli et al.\ propose a TinyML compiler that targets C with LUT-based
activations~\cite{Cerioli2025}.

\subsection{Compressed and efficient sequence models}
Kusupati et al.\ introduce FastGRNN for MCU-class
inference~\cite{Kusupati2018}.
Kannan and Hoffmann propose Budget RNNs for multiple energy
budgets~\cite{Kannan2021}.
Cheng et al.\ present dynamic gated recurrent networks to reduce
computation~\cite{Cheng2024}.

\subsection{Hardware accelerators}
Lee et al.\ propose an FPGA-based RNN accelerator~\cite{Lee2021}.
Chen et al.\ present Eciton, a low-power FPGA accelerator for edge
nodes~\cite{Chen2024}.
Muzamal et al.\ propose a reconfigurable MLP--RNN processor with LUT-based
multipliers~\cite{Muzamal2021}.
Lin et al.\ introduce LUTIN, converting matvecs to table lookups for low-bit
inference~\cite{Lin2024}.

\subsection{LUT-based runtime optimization}
Rezk et al.\ review LUT-based nonlinearities in RNN
deployments~\cite{Rezk2020}.
Rusci et al.\ propose mixed FP16--INT8 quantization with LUT-backed activations
for MCU speech enhancement~\cite{Rusci2022}.
Alvanaki investigates DVFS and data layout for energy-efficient TinyML on
STM32 MCUs~\cite{Alvanaki2024}.

\subsection{GPU-targeted LLM quantization}
GPTQ~\cite{frantar2023gptq} applies one-shot weight quantization using
approximate second-order information, achieving 4-bit compression of large
LLMs (e.g., OPT-125M, WikiText-2 PPL\,=\,31.12) on GPU.
SqueezeLLM~\cite{kim2024squeezellm} combines dense-and-sparse quantization with
non-uniform codebooks, attaining PPL\,=\,5.79 at 3.45 bits for LLaMA-7B on GPU.
LLM.int8()~\cite{dettmers2022llmint8} uses mixed-precision decomposition for
8-bit matrix multiplication at scale.
AWQ~\cite{lin2024awq} applies activation-aware weight quantization to 4-bit,
preserving channels with high activation magnitude.
These methods target GPU inference and either require VRAM or dense
dequantization; they do not provide streaming-from-Flash MCU execution.

\subsection{Gap with respect to CTLE}
No prior work jointly provides:
(a) a tensor-wise 4-bit codebook-plus-index representation
\emph{learned via metaheuristics} (GA/PSO) from each tensor's weight
distribution;
(b) direct streaming execution on MCU-class hardware in C without intermediate
weight-expansion buffers; and
(c) a multi-format firmware engine that enables fair on-device comparison
between LUT-codebook and standard INT4 baselines on the same RISC-V MCU.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{CTLE representation: codebook and 4-bit indices}
\label{subsec:representation}

Consider a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$.
CTLE replaces $\mathbf{W}$ by:
(i) a codebook $\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$, $K=16$, and
(ii) an index matrix $\mathbf{I}\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}^{M\times N}$.
The quantizer is
\begin{equation}
Q(w,\mathcal{L}) \triangleq
\mathcal{L}\!\left[\arg\min_{k} |w - \mathcal{L}[k]|\right].
\label{eq:quantizer}
\end{equation}
Codebook learning minimizes mean squared reconstruction error:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}
\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{m,n}\Bigl(\mathbf{W}_{m,n}-Q(\mathbf{W}_{m,n},\mathcal{L})\Bigr)^2.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
We apply CTLE to all large tensors (token embedding and $W_q,W_k,W_v,W_o,
W_1,W_2,W_3$ per layer); small tensors (RMSNorm gains) are kept in FP32.

\subsection{K-means codebook learning}
\label{subsec:kmeans}

Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} is solved by 1D K-means on the flattened weight
values ($N_\mathrm{iter}=300$, percentile initialization across $K$ quantiles).
This is fast ($<$2\,s per tensor) and provides the warm-start codebook for GA
and PSO.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm codebook optimization (CTLE+GA)}
\label{subsec:ga}

We apply a steady-state GA to refine the K-means codebook.
Each individual is a codebook vector $\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^K$ with fitness
equal to negative reconstruction MSE on a random subsample of at most
50{,}000 weights (stochastic fitness evaluation).

\paragraph{Operators.}
\textit{Selection}: binary tournament ($k=3$).
\textit{Crossover}: BLX-$\alpha$ ($\alpha=0.3$, $p_\mathrm{cross}=0.8$):
for each coordinate $j$, $\ell_j^{\mathrm{child}} = u$ where $u$ is sampled
uniformly from
$[\min(\ell_j^{p_1},\ell_j^{p_2})-\alpha\delta_j,\,
   \max(\ell_j^{p_1},\ell_j^{p_2})+\alpha\delta_j]$,
$\delta_j=|\ell_j^{p_1}-\ell_j^{p_2}|$.
\textit{Mutation}: Gaussian perturbation of random codebook entries
($p_\mathrm{mut}=0.15$, $\sigma=0.05\,\sigma_\mathbf{W}$).
\textit{Elitism}: top-1 individual carried into next generation.

\paragraph{Numba acceleration.}
The hot fitness loop (\textit{assign} + \textit{MSE}) is compiled with
\texttt{@njit(cache=True)}, reducing runtime from $\sim$hours to $\sim$208\,s
for the full 15M-parameter model on a laptop CPU.

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimization codebook (CTLE+PSO)}
\label{subsec:pso}

PSO maintains a swarm of $N_p=60$ particles, each encoding a codebook
$\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^K$. Particles are initialized from K-means warm start
with random perturbation ($\sigma=0.1\,\sigma_\mathbf{W}$).

\paragraph{Update rule.}
At iteration $t$, each particle $i$ updates velocity $\mathbf{v}_i$ and
position $\mathbf{p}_i$ as
\begin{align}
\mathbf{v}_i^{t+1} &= \omega_t \mathbf{v}_i^{t}
  + c_1 r_1 (\mathbf{p}_i^\mathrm{best} - \mathbf{p}_i^{t})
  + c_2 r_2 (\mathbf{g}^\mathrm{best} - \mathbf{p}_i^{t}), \\
\mathbf{p}_i^{t+1} &= \mathbf{p}_i^{t} + \mathbf{v}_i^{t+1},
\end{align}
with $c_1 = c_2 = 2.0$, $r_1,r_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$, and
linearly decaying inertia $\omega_t = \omega_\mathrm{max} -
(\omega_\mathrm{max}-\omega_\mathrm{min})\,t/(T-1)$,
$\omega_\mathrm{max}=0.9$, $\omega_\mathrm{min}=0.4$.
Velocities are clipped to $\pm v_\mathrm{max}=0.1\,(\max\mathbf{W}-\min\mathbf{W})$.
Fitness is evaluated on a 50{,}000-weight random subsample.
The hot kernel is compiled with Numba JIT, achieving $\sim$198\,s total for the
full model.

\subsection{4-bit nibble packing and Flash layout}
\label{subsec:packing}

Two consecutive indices $\eta_0,\eta_1\in\{0,\dots,15\}$ are packed into one
byte:
\begin{equation}
\beta = \eta_0 \;\lor\; (\eta_1 \ll 4),
\end{equation}
requiring $\lceil MN/2\rceil$ bytes per matrix.
Each CTLE block is serialized as
$\bigl[\mathcal{L}\ \Vert\ \mathbf{I}_\mathrm{pack}\bigr]$
(16 FP32 + packed bytes), concatenated in a fixed tensor order with a 40-byte
header (magic, version, model dimensions, flags).

\subsection{CTLE inference engine}
\label{subsec:engine}

The matrix--vector product is computed without expanding $\mathbf{W}$:
\begin{equation}
y_m = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mathcal{L}\!\left[I_{m,n}\right] x_n,
\label{eq:matvec}
\end{equation}
streaming packed bytes and decoding two indices per byte in the hot loop
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctle}). The 16-entry LUT (64\,B) fits in CPU registers.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CTLE fused Tensor--LUT kernel ($N$ even).}
\label{alg:ctle}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^N$, packed $\mathbf{P}$, codebook
$\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$
\ENSURE $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^M$
\FOR{$m = 0$ \textbf{to} $M-1$}
    \STATE $\mathrm{acc} \gets 0$
    \FOR{$j = 0$ \textbf{to} $N/2-1$}
        \STATE $B \gets \mathbf{P}[m,j]$
        \STATE $\mathrm{acc} \gets \mathrm{acc}
               + x_{2j}\cdot\mathcal{L}[B\,\&\,\mathtt{0x0F}]
               + x_{2j+1}\cdot\mathcal{L}[B\!\gg\!4]$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE $y_m \gets \mathrm{acc}$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{INT4 baselines (our implementation)}
\label{subsec:int4baselines}

For a fair comparison we implement two standard INT4 baselines in the same
Python pipeline and C firmware.

\paragraph{INT4 Uniform (TAG\_INT4U).}
One symmetric scale per tensor:
$s = \max|\mathbf{W}|/7 + \epsilon$,
$q_{m,n} = \mathrm{clip}(\mathrm{round}(w_{m,n}/s), -7, 7)$,
stored as unsigned nibble $q+7\in[0,14]$.
Storage: $4 + \lceil MN/2\rceil$ bytes.

\paragraph{INT4 Block-wise (TAG\_INT4BW).}
One symmetric scale per row-group of $G=32$ columns:
$s_{m,g} = \max_{c\in\mathcal{G}(m,g)}|w_{m,c}|/7 + \epsilon$.
Storage: $4MN_g + \lceil MN/2\rceil$ bytes,
$N_g=\lceil N/G\rceil$ (effective $\approx5$\,bits/weight).

\paragraph{Multi-format C firmware.}
\texttt{WeightBlock} stores tag, nibble pointer, and---depending on format---a
16-entry LUT (CTLE), a scalar scale (INT4U), or a scale array
\texttt{scales[rows$\times$n\_groups]} (INT4BW).
Dispatch in \texttt{matvec()} selects the appropriate kernel at runtime.

\subsection{Autoregressive execution on ESP32-P4 Nano}
\label{subsec:hw}

The firmware implements a Llama-2 decoder: RMSNorm, CTLE matvec for all
projections ($W_q,W_k,W_v,W_o,W_1,W_2,W_3$), RoPE (precomputed
$\sin/\cos$ tables), causal KV-cache in PSRAM, SwiGLU FFN, and
tied-weight output projection.
The model is stored on SPIFFS (partition at 0x310000), loaded into PSRAM at
boot ($\sim$2.7\,s), and benchmarked with three hardcoded prompts.
Sampling uses temperature\,=\,0.8 and top-$p$\,=\,0.9.

\section{Comparative Analysis of Quantization Schemes}
\label{sec:analysis}

We compare four 4-bit representations for computing $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$
on MCU-class hardware.

\paragraph{A. INT4 Uniform (tensor-wise scale).}
$y_m = s\sum_n q_{m,n}x_n$ with a single per-tensor scale $s$.
Simple and fast but severely limited by the non-uniform weight distribution of
large embedding matrices.

\paragraph{B. INT4 Block-wise (group-wise scale).}
$y_m = \sum_g s_{m,g}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{G}(m,g)}q_{m,n}x_n$.
Better fidelity at the cost of per-group scale reads and FP32 conversion in the
hot loop.
Scale storage: $M\lceil N/G\rceil\!\cdot\!4$ bytes (FP32 in our implementation).

\paragraph{C. K-means codebook with expansion (expand-then-compute).}
Reconstruct dense $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ then multiply.
Requires $4MN$ bytes of transient SRAM---infeasible for large tensors on MCUs.

\paragraph{D. CTLE fused Tensor--LUT (proposed).}
Decodes indices on-the-fly (Eq.~\ref{eq:matvec}).
Transient memory: $\mathcal{O}(1)$ (accumulator + 64-byte LUT in registers).
Compressed storage: $\lceil MN/2\rceil + 64$ bytes.
Eliminates scale tables, dequantization paths, and weight-expansion buffers.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model.}
TinyStories-15M (Llama-2 architecture,
$d\!=\!288$, $n_\mathrm{layers}\!=\!6$, $n_\mathrm{heads}\!=\!6$,
$d_\mathrm{FFN}\!=\!768$, $\mathrm{vocab}\!=\!32\,000$);
weights from the karpathy/tinyllamas HuggingFace
repository~\cite{karpathy2023llama2c}.

\paragraph{Evaluation corpus.}
10 TinyStories-style sentences, same for all methods.
NLL is mean cross-entropy per token; $\mathrm{PPL}=\exp(\mathrm{NLL})$.

\paragraph{Compression pipeline.}
Script \texttt{compress.py}, argument \texttt{--method} $\in$
\texttt{\{kmeans, ga, pso, int4\_uniform, int4\_blockwise\}};
all runs use \texttt{--k~16}, seed\,=\,42.
Compression timing was measured on an Apple M-series laptop (no GPU).

\paragraph{Hardware target.}
ESP32-P4 Nano: RISC-V HP core @360\,MHz (silicon rev\,v1.3 hardware cap;
SDK configured for 400\,MHz), 32\,MB QSPI PSRAM, 16\,MB Flash, ESP-IDF v6.1.

\subsection{Quantization ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares all five compression schemes.
INT4 Uniform collapses catastrophically (PPL\,=\,11070): a single global scale
is unable to represent the dynamic range of the 9.2M-entry embedding matrix
(32\,000$\times$288 weights), which spans multiple orders of magnitude.
INT4 Block-wise ($G\!=\!32$) recovers quality (PPL\,=\,10.71) but increases
footprint by 25\,\% (9.51\,MB vs.\ 7.61\,MB) and requires loading and
converting a per-group scale table during inference.

CTLE+K-means achieves PPL\,=\,19.23 at 7.61\,MB (7.98$\times$) with
compression completed in 74\,s.
CTLE+GA marginally improves to PPL\,=\,18.98 in 208\,s.
CTLE+PSO achieves the best CTLE result (PPL\,=\,15.93, $-$17.2\,\% vs.\
K-means) in 198\,s, driven entirely by better optimization of the embedding
tensor (Table~\ref{tab:relmse}).

\paragraph{Memory.}
All 32\,MB of PSRAM are available at boot.
RunState (KV-cache $+$ RoPE tables $+$ activation struct) consumes 3{,}648\,KB
and is identical across formats.
Model weights occupy 7{,}551\,KB (INT4U/CTLE) or 9{,}435\,KB (INT4BW) in PSRAM;
the heap allocator adds a small alignment overhead relative to the on-disk size.

be deployed on-device.
Internal SRAM (624\,KB total) remains 570\,KB free throughout inference;
the 54\,KB used by the IDF runtime is constant across formats.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{INT4U vs.\ CTLE.}
Uniform INT4 with a single global scale fails catastrophically on transformers
whose weight distribution is multimodal and heavy-tailed.
This result---PPL collapse from 6.92 to 11{,}070---is consistent with prior work
on per-tensor INT4 baselines~\cite{frantar2023gptq,tomasev2019llm_quantization_survey},
and explains why per-group schemes (INT4BW) or learned codebooks (CTLE) are
necessary for sub-8-bit compression.

\paragraph{INT4BW vs.\ CTLE: quality--throughput trade-off.}
INT4BW achieves better PPL (10.71 vs.\ 15.93) by using finer-grained scale
adaptation, at the cost of 25\,\% higher storage (9.51 vs.\ 7.61\,MB).
The hardware benchmark reveals a second, qualitatively different cost:
INT4BW is \textbf{31\,\%} slower than CTLE on the same hardware
($1{,}356$ vs.\ $1{,}036$\,ms/tok), a gap that arises purely from PSRAM
bandwidth.
Each INT4BW matvec row requires reading
$\lceil N/G \rceil = \lceil 288/32 \rceil = 9$ per-group scale values from
the \texttt{scales[rows$\times$n\_groups]} table in PSRAM before processing
the nibble stream, effectively doubling PSRAM memory transactions per row.
INT4U eliminates this cost by using one scalar scale per tensor (cached in a
register), and CTLE replaces it with a 16-float LUT that fits in 16 registers
and remains resident throughout the entire row loop---neither format requires
additional PSRAM reads beyond the nibble stream itself.
As a result, INT4U and CTLE achieve statistically indistinguishable throughput
despite different dequantisation arithmetic (scalar multiply vs.\ LUT lookup).
The choice between INT4BW and CTLE+PSO therefore depends on the deployment
constraint: INT4BW is preferable when PPL is the primary metric and the
31\,\% throughput penalty is acceptable; CTLE+PSO is better when maximum
throughput and minimum footprint take priority.

\paragraph{Value of PSO over K-means.}
The $-$17.2\,\% PPL improvement of PSO over K-means at zero additional memory
cost demonstrates that metaheuristic codebook optimization is a viable and
practical technique for TinyLM compression.
The gain is concentrated on the embedding matrix (9.2M weights, two orders of
magnitude larger than any other tensor), confirming that K-means local minima
are most harmful for large, non-Gaussian distributions.
On small tensors ($\leq$82K weights), all three methods converge to the same
quality, validating that K-means already finds the global minimum for those.

\paragraph{Throughput on ESP32-P4.}
The measured $0.965\pm0.001$\,tok/s for CTLE and $0.961\pm0.001$\,tok/s for
INT4U on the ESP32-P4 Nano RISC-V@360\,MHz are baselines without
RISC-V-specific kernel tuning.
Both INT4U and CTLE are dominated by PSRAM bandwidth---reading the nibble
stream---rather than arithmetic, explaining their near-identical throughput.
INT4BW, by contrast, adds a second PSRAM access pattern (scale reads) that
increases effective memory traffic per matvec row, reducing throughput by 31\,\%
independently of the nibble bandwidth.
The dominant compute cost per token is the output logit projection
(32\,000$\times$288 matvec), with the six attention and six FFN layers sharing
the remaining time.
RISC-V-specific optimisations (RVV vectorised nibble decode, unrolled LUT
scatter-accumulate) are expected to reduce the per-token time and are left for
future work.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
First, evaluation is on a single model and corpus; broader testing across
tasks and model families is needed.
Second, CTLE uses one codebook per matrix; group-wise codebooks or
mixed-precision extensions may improve quality.
Third, energy was not measured on the P4 (hardware instrumentation pending);
the 0.965\,tok/s result provides throughput only.
Fourth, WikiText-2 as a second evaluation dataset is planned but not yet
included in this version.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented CTLE, a 4-bit weight-sharing inference engine for IoT
microcontrollers that streams packed indices from Flash and performs
lookup-and-accumulate matrix--vector products without materializing dense
weight tensors in SRAM.
Three codebook learning strategies were compared: K-means, GA, and PSO.
PSO achieves the best quality ($-$17.2\,\% PPL vs.\ K-means) at identical
7.98$\times$ compression, through better optimization of the 9.2M-entry
embedding tensor.
INT4 Uniform collapses (PPL\,=\,11{,}070), demonstrating that per-group or
learned scales are necessary for 4-bit transformers.
INT4 Block-wise achieves better PPL (10.71) at 25\,\% higher footprint and with
per-group scale overhead.

On the ESP32-P4 Nano (RISC-V HP@360\,MHz), CTLE+PSO achieves 0.965\,tok/s,
loading a 7.61-MB model from SPIFFS in 2.69\,s and leaving 21.5\,MB of
PSRAM available for the KV-cache, RoPE tables, and application state.
Coherent TinyStories-style text is generated for all three benchmark prompts,
confirming that 4-bit CTLE+PSO preserves practical generation quality.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
(i) RISC-V-optimized CTLE kernel (RVV extension, unrolled nibble decoding).
(ii) WikiText-2 evaluation for broader generalizability.
(iii) Energy measurement on ESP32-P4 with hardware power meter.
(iv) CTLE-NPU: a Verilog systolic-array implementation on Cyclone IV FPGA
     with PicoRV32, reusing the GA/PSO codebook optimizer with hardware-aware
     fitness (INT8 fixed-point penalty)---target DATE/FPL/TCAS-II.
(v) Group-wise codebooks or sparse residuals for improved quality at 4 bits.
(vi) Codebook learning guided by validation loss (task-aware optimization).
